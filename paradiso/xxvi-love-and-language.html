Love and the First Language

Dante's sight is clouded by the brilliance of Saint John, but John continues the third examination. What is the true object of love, and why is it worthy of love?

Dante answers that God is the supreme good, and that all other worthy loves take their measure from that source. Scripture, philosophy, and the experience of goodness help him explain why.

The light can be beyond sight, and love can still orient the will.

Dante's sight returns. A fourth soul joins Peter, James, and John: Adam. Dante asks when Adam was created, how long he lived in Eden, what caused his expulsion, and what language he spoke.

Adam's answer is startling: human language changes over time. The name used for God before Babel was not the same as the later name. Words are human conventions that vary with custom and history.

The natural boundary

Meaning and its linguistic representation are not identical. Names can change while speakers continue referring to a shared object. Equally, matching labels do not guarantee matching meaning.

Adam also explains that the forbidden act was not mere tasting, but the transgression of a boundary. In the poem the fall concerns will and obedience, not simply the fruit as an object.
