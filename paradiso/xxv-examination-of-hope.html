Paradiso XXV · Fixed Stars · Natural Boundary 025

The Examination of Hope

Dante imagines returning one day to Florence, the city that exiled him, and receiving a poet's laurel at the baptismal font where he entered the faith. The longing is intensely human.

Then another radiant soul approaches: Saint James. Beatrice invites him to examine Dante on hope, the second theological virtue.

Hope looks toward a promised good without claiming to possess it already.

Saint James asks three questions: What is hope? How fully does Dante possess it? And from where did it come?

Dante describes hope as a confident expectation of future glory, rooted in divine grace and previous merit in the poem's theological framework. He traces its teaching through the Psalms and James's own epistle. Beatrice answers the question about his degree of hope on his behalf.

Then Saint John arrives in radiant brilliance. Dante strains to see whether John has brought his body to heaven. John corrects him: in the poem, only Christ and Mary are there bodily at this stage. Dante's sight is temporarily overwhelmed.

The natural boundary

Expectation is not possession. A future good can orient action without being falsely recorded as a present observation.
