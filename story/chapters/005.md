## The River That Carries Voices

The boat made no sound as it approached.

Its hull cut the surface of Acheron, leaving a seam so dark that Dante could not tell where water ended and the underworld began. Charon stood in the prow, one hand wrapped around a long pole, his red eyes fixed upon the living traveler.

Dante heard the cries before he saw the passengers.

At first they sounded like a storm heard through stone. Then individual words emerged: a lost child's name, an apology without its listener, a promise made too late. None of the voices agreed on what ought to be remembered.

“May I listen?” Dante whispered.

“You may,” Virgil replied. “But do not claim to know a soul from one word carried across a river.”

So Dante listened without answering.

A voice called for home. Another asked whether morning still came to Florence. An old man named a garden, then forgot what flower he had grown there. From the far shore came a long silence, so complete that Dante could hear his own breath.

The living poet understood something terrible and tender: sorrow was not made smaller by being counted, and a thousand cries were still a thousand separate stories.

Charon raised his pole.

“A living one? Not here!”

Virgil stepped forward and answered with the authority of a road already decreed. Charon's anger did not vanish, but he turned the boat.

The shore trembled. Darkness rose around Dante like a wave. He reached toward the sound of Virgil's voice, and the world fell away.

When sight returned, he stood beyond the river.

There was no blaze before him—only a wide, hushed country beneath a dim sky. A castle of gentle light glimmered in the distance. Its walls seemed built not against enemies but against forgetting.

Virgil looked toward it with the gravity of someone returning home.

“This,” he said, “is Limbo.”

Dante listened again. Here there were no screams, only longing.
