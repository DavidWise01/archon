## The Book and the Wind

The storm did not blow in gusts. It circled without fatigue, carrying souls like leaves through an autumn that could never end.

Virgil raised one hand. “Those two who travel together—call to them gently.”

Dante called, and the lovers drifted nearer. The woman spoke; the man beside her wept silently.

“I am Francesca,” she said. “And this is Paolo.”

“What brought you here?”

“A book,” Francesca answered, “and what we found waiting within ourselves.”

They had been reading the romance of Lancelot and Guinevere. The words described a kiss, and the readers looked away from the page and toward each other. In that instant, the story and their own unspoken desire seemed to become the same thing.

“The book did not command us,” Francesca said. “It gave our longing a shape.”

The wind drew tight around her, but she continued.

“We read no further that day.”

Paolo's tears slipped into the dark and were taken by the storm.

Dante remembered his choice in Limbo: to notice the limits of wisdom. A story could reveal a heart, he thought, but neither the beauty of a book nor the gentleness of a voice made every choice innocent.

He wanted to pity the lovers without replacing their history with one of his own making.

Francesca reached toward the poets as the current turned.

“Remember us,” she said.

Then the storm reclaimed them.

Dante stood motionless until Virgil guided him toward the lower passage. The wind grew cold and wet. Its long circling sound broke apart into falling drops. Air that had held bodies aloft became a weight pressing everything downward.

Below lay a field of black mud beneath an endless rain. A three-headed shape moved in the dark, and farther away a man half-buried in mire raised his face.

“Ciacco,” Virgil murmured. “And Cerberus guards the circle.”

Dante listened to the heavy rain.
