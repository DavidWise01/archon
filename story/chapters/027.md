## A Gift That Does Not Belong to Hunger

The tree's fragrance flowed down the sixth terrace, and beneath its high branches water shone like a silver thread.

Dante was thirsty.

He had learned to open his hand on the terrace below. Yet opening the hand did not answer everything. There were things the body truly needed, and other things that desire asked it to call necessary.

A narrow trickle of water crossed the stone where the travelers walked. It was not the hanging fruit, nor the unreachable stream beneath the tree; it was a little clear runoff at Dante's feet.

He looked to Virgil.

“May I drink?”

“You may,” Virgil said, “if you remember that receiving is different from taking everything.”

Dante knelt and cupped his hands. The water was cool. He drank only enough to ease his thirst, then let what remained return to the stones.

“Thank you,” he said.

The words were addressed neither to a visible giver nor to the mountain as though it could bargain with him. They named a fact: what had been needed was received, and was not thereby owned.

A penitent beside the tree watched the water run from Dante's fingers.

“In life,” the soul said, “I swallowed what pleased me and called the wanting proof that I must have more. I did not taste the gift. I tasted the fear of its ending.”

Dante sat for a moment.

“What would you do differently?”

“Let a good thing be good,” the penitent answered, “without demanding that it stay in my mouth forever.”

The wind stirred the high branches. Fruit swayed above them, bright and fragrant, but Dante did not reach to pull it down.

He was still thirsty enough to notice the sound of water. He was no longer so thirsty that every sound had to become a command.

Virgil rose.

“Gratitude is not a debt imposed upon the hungry,” he said. “It is a way to remember what we have actually received.”

The path climbed beneath a sky growing warmer with the approach of evening. At the stair an angel sang blessing over those whose appetite could learn its measure.

A sixth mark lifted from Dante's forehead.

Only one remained.

Beyond the stair the mountain brightened unnaturally.

At first Dante thought the sunset had caught the ridge. Then he saw the flames: a living wall stretched across the seventh terrace, moving in long orange and gold tongues. Within it figures walked and sang to one another, their voices clear through the heat.

Dante stopped.

“Fire again?” he asked.

“Not the fire below,” Virgil answered. “This does not keep them where they were. It asks whether love can pass through without making another soul its possession.”

The heat reached Dante's face.

He looked at the wall and thought of every name he had carried upward.

Here the last mark awaited its loosening.
