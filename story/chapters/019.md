## The Sky That Was Never Lost

When Dante stepped out of the narrow throat of the earth, the air felt too large to hold in his lungs.

He had crossed storms, marshes, fire, fraud, and a lake where even tears could not fall. He had passed the still center of the world and found that the same step could mean descent on one side and ascent on the other.

Now the stars were above him.

He might have hurried toward the shore. Instead he stopped, raised his eyes, and began to count.

“One,” he whispered.

A small light near the edge of the dark.

“Two.”

A second near it, distant enough that the space between them could not be measured with any step he possessed.

Virgil watched without interrupting.

Dante counted until the constellations became too crowded for his tally. Some lights seemed solitary; others formed patterns only because of where he stood. He knew the patterns could help a traveler remember a direction, but he had learned not to mistake a pattern for possession of its parts.

“Have these been here all along?” Dante asked.

“They were not absent because you could not see them,” Virgil said.

Dante thought of the imprisoned faces beneath the ice. Even there, beyond the stone and the world’s cold weight, these lights had continued. Their shining had not shortened anyone’s suffering. It had not turned injustice into kindness. It simply remained true outside the narrow horizon of the pit.

“I cannot count them all,” Dante said.

“You have counted what you can see. That is a beginning, not a failure.”

The horizon lightened almost imperceptibly. The black sea took on a long thread of silver; then lavender, then a faint rose. Dante held the last stars in memory as the sky changed around them.

He marked no victory over darkness. The stars had not been defeated by day. They were merely hidden by a greater light.

Down the shore a mountain rose, its upper terraces still veiled by the early morning. Near the water stood a stern figure whose gaze seemed older than the shore itself.

“Cato,” Virgil said. “He watches the approach to Purgatory.”

The long descent had ended. Ahead was another kind of journey: not the discovery of what could not be changed, but the work of becoming different.

Dante took one last look at the fading stars.

He could not bring the sky with him. He could carry the habit of looking.
