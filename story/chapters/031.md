## The Measure That Was Not a Ladder

The Moon shone like polished silver beneath Dante's feet.

What he first mistook for reflections slowly became faces, peaceful and luminous. Among them stood Piccarda Donati.

“Why do you remain in this lower heaven?” Dante asked. “Do you not desire a place nearer the highest light?”

Piccarda smiled. “You imagine that distance measures happiness, because you have learned to measure earthly things that way.”

She told him how she had been removed from religious life against her will. Her outward promise had been interrupted by the actions of others, yet that violence had not defined the entirety of her inward faith.

Dante listened.

He remembered the stone-bearers on Pride's terrace, the penitents whose eyes were closed against envy, and the lesson he had carried through Lethe.

The place a soul occupies is not a simple measure of its worth.

“Would you choose a higher place?” he asked.

Piccarda's answer was gentle. “Our peace is found in willing what divine love wills.”

Beatrice watched Dante receive the answer.

For once, he did not immediately compare one person against another. He allowed Piccarda's testimony to remain her own.

When the moonlight began to dissolve around them, Dante understood that his next ascent would not be measured only by how far he traveled. It would also be measured by what he ceased demanding of those he encountered.

“Then I must learn to ask,” Dante said, “before I presume to judge.”

The spheres brightened. Mercury lay ahead, where noble deeds and the desire for recognition would complicate his understanding of intention.
