## The Man Who Answered Fire

Fire descended upon the desert like red snow.

Some souls ran beneath it, their footsteps tracing frantic paths in the sand. Others crouched and tried to make themselves small. Near the center of the plain lay one man who would not bend or move even as the embers struck him.

Dante approached along the narrow edge of the burning ground.

“Who speaks against the sky?” he asked.

The man raised his head. “Capaneus. I spoke against heaven before you came, and I shall speak against it when you leave.”

A flame settled on his shoulder. He did not brush it aside.

“Does it lessen the pain,” Dante asked, “to refuse what hurts you?”

“Let the fire learn that I refuse it.”

Dante listened. The words were tremendous, but he could hear how little changed between them. The sky did not answer the challenge. The sand still burned. Capaneus turned his voice upward again, as if volume might become a key to a locked door.

Virgil stepped between Dante and the deepest heat.

“You have heard him,” the guide said. “His fury belongs to him. You do not need to mistake his defiance for freedom.”

Capaneus laughed. “You come to judge my courage?”

“No,” Dante answered. “I came to hear what you would say.”

He wanted to ask whether there had ever been another word Capaneus could have chosen. But the answer was already underway—the same challenge, repeated into the falling fire.

Dante remembered the processions of stones. Here too a life had become a motion it would not interrupt.

Virgil drew him toward a narrow stream running red between banks of baked stone. Its steam made a small passage through the shower of embers. They followed its course toward a broken rim.

At the edge, the earth fell away into a vast arrangement of circular trenches. Dark bridges arched from one ring to the next; some spanned the abyss, and some stopped midway in fractured air.

Far below, figures moved in lines, robes glinted with false gold, and distant voices promised what they could not give.

“Malebolge,” Virgil said. “The eighth circle. Here deceit makes roads that seem sound until someone stands upon them.”

Dante looked down at the broken bridges.

After the fire, even a bridge needed to be examined before it could be trusted.
