## The Edge of the Lantern

The castle shone without fire.

Its seven walls held a quiet green meadow, and beneath its arches the voices of old poets traveled as gently as rain. Virgil greeted them without ceremony. Their words had survived kingdoms, shipwrecks, ruined libraries, and men who thought silence could erase a name.

Dante stood before a lantern that burned with no smoke.

“Are these the wisest people who ever lived?” he asked.

“They knew much,” Virgil replied. “That does not mean they knew everything.”

Dante heard the distant singing. There was no torment in it, yet neither was there the joy of arrival.

“Then what failed them?”

Virgil looked toward the low sky.

“Wisdom can name the stars, measure the sea, and teach a frightened traveler to reason. Yet a map is not the country. A correct word is not the whole of mercy. Knowledge has edges. You are standing at one.”

Dante touched the lantern's iron frame. Its light fell only so far. Beyond the pale circle, the meadow returned to shadow.

He thought of the she-wolf and her endless hunger. To know a thing's name was not to know how to change it. To watch was not to own what one had seen.

Dante bowed to the poets. He would keep their voices, but would not put words in their mouths.

Virgil led him through the castle's last arch.

The gentle air thinned. A sound rose below them: the drawn-out groan of a door, a judge's terrible tail striking stone, then the roar of wind upon wind.

The next chamber was not quiet.

Souls flew through its darkness like leaves caught in a storm without autumn. Far within the turning air, two figures held close to one another, unable to find the ground.

“Circle Two,” Virgil said. “Listen before you judge them.”

Dante placed one hand over the little lantern he had carried in memory. It did not stop the wind. It gave him just enough light to see the path.
