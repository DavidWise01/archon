## The Distance Between Towers

Dante stood at the Giant Well and tried to determine how far the floor lay below.

The rim gave him no trustworthy answer. A pebble knocked loose from the stone fell until its sound became too small to hear. The shaft was not simply dark; its distance overwhelmed the measures by which he knew a road.

He chose a mark on the nearest giant's shoulder and measured it against the span of the broken bridge. What he had taken for a tower moved when the giant breathed. The dark belt around its waist passed into the rock like a wall around a city.

“Can we descend by counting?” Dante asked.

“Count what you can,” Virgil answered. “But do not give a number to what you have not observed.”

Dante counted the steps remaining at the rim, the pace between two protruding stones, the length of one colossal finger. Even the finger seemed longer than the route that had brought them to this edge.

He tried again, comparing a hand to a forearm, a shoulder to the breadth of the well. The proportions changed each time the immense figure shifted. A line of sight was not a ladder.

“I can measure pieces,” Dante said. “I cannot make them a path.”

“Then your measure has taught you something true.”

One of the giants muttered in a tongue Dante could not interpret. Another stood chained. Beyond them Antaeus bent his great frame, and the moving shadow of his hand crossed the rim.

Virgil called to him. The giant reached downward, palm open like a platform above an immeasurable drop.

Dante watched the hand stop. He could estimate its breadth, but he could not force it into the scale of an ordinary step.

He stepped beside Virgil.

Antaeus lifted them from the rim and lowered them through the throat of the earth. Stone passed upward on every side. For a moment Dante lost all sense of which distance belonged to his body and which to the world.

Then the giant's hand touched a surface that gave no sound.

It was not stone.

It was ice.

The cold rose through Dante's shoes. Around him, pale faces lay trapped beneath the frozen plain, their eyes open toward a sky they could not reach. The very bottom of the underworld was not a furnace but a lake held motionless.

“Cocytus,” Virgil said. “The ninth circle.”

Dante had expected a fire deeper than every fire above.

Instead, he saw stillness so severe that even tears could freeze.

He took one careful step and listened for a crack in the ice.
