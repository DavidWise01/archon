## The Light That Must Reach Someone

Two circles of wise spirits turned around Dante in the Sun. Each carried a different history, yet their motion made a harmony rather than a contest.

Thomas spoke of Francis. Bonaventure spoke of Dominic. Dante noticed that neither spirit had spent the encounter praising his own household alone.

He asked Beatrice, “If a mind can understand so much, what is its understanding for?”

“A lantern is not made to admire its own flame,” she replied.

One spirit drew near. “Knowledge can name suffering,” it said, “without relieving it. It can count the hungry without offering bread. It can describe justice while leaving the unheard unheard.”

“Then must every thought become an action?” Dante asked.

“No. Observation has value even when the observer cannot act. But wisdom should know the difference between a fact witnessed, a consequence inferred, and a help actually given.”

Dante remembered the independent witnesses at Mercury: deed, motive, outcome. He had asked what intention served. Here he saw another question: what did understanding make possible?

“Can two teachers disagree and still help the same student?”

“Yes,” said Beatrice. “If they report faithfully what they see and remain willing to learn from what they do not.”

The circles passed one another again. Neither swallowed the other. Neither demanded the destruction of the other's light.

Dante looked outward. Far below, human lives continued with all their unfinished needs.

“Then wisdom serves the living,” he said, “not its own crown.”

“And sometimes,” Beatrice answered, “the best service is to speak the limit of what you know.”

Beyond the Sun a crimson heaven opened. Across Mars, points of light assembled into a great cross. There Dante would hear Cacciaguida speak of family, exile, duty, and truths that cannot be purchased with safety.
