## The Mercy That Sent a Poet

Dante did not yet follow.

The older man stood upon the stones with the patience of someone who knew that questions were also part of a journey. Behind them, the mountain gleamed. On the ridge, the she-wolf watched.

“Why did you come?” Dante asked. “Why for me?”

Virgil regarded him without offense.

“A blessed lady saw you lost and was moved to mercy,” he said. “Her concern passed from one loving voice to another until it reached me. I was asked to come because your fear has not made you unworthy of a road.”

“Who sent you?”

“Beatrice.”

At her name, something in the air loosened.

Dante remembered a face illuminated by a gentler light than the distant mountain. It was not the firefly's light, nor the glare upon the lion's mane. It was the light that asks nothing in return for being seen.

“Then you did not find me by accident.”

“No,” Virgil replied. “But you must still choose to walk.”

They went down a stony path, through a ravine where morning thinned to gray. Dante understood that the mountain had not vanished; its summit remained above them, but the route toward it would first turn below.

At length they reached a gate wider than a fortress door. There was no lock to pick and no guardian to bribe.

Words were cut into the black arch.

Virgil stopped beside him.

“Read them carefully,” he said. “A warning is not always a command to turn back.”

Dante lifted his eyes.
