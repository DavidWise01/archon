## What Deserves the Hour

On the fourth terrace the penitents ran as though the mountain itself were calling them forward.

Their feet struck stone in a continuous rhythm. They called examples of urgent love to one another: an errand undertaken without hesitation, a promise kept before it cooled, a kindness not delayed until it became convenient.

Dante felt the urge to join them.

It would have been simple to run, to borrow the shape of their purpose and mistake speed for intention.

Instead he turned to Virgil.

“Name a worthy task,” Dante said. “What should be done while the hour is ours?”

Virgil watched a group of runners pass.

“The mountain does not reward motion alone. Choose what you have seen needs doing.”

Dante thought of the penitent whose stone he had helped bear. He thought of voices that had taught him to rejoice in someone else's good, and of the unseen soul he had answered softly in the smoke.

“I will listen when another is speaking,” he said. “I will not make their burden an exhibit, or their joy a contest, or their uncertainty a threat.”

Virgil waited.

Dante understood the difference between naming a virtue and naming a task.

“Then here is the task: at the next place where I can help, I will ask what is needed and offer what I can actually give.”

“Good,” said Virgil. “Let the task meet the world before you call it accomplished.”

The runners passed again. One penitent heard Dante and called back:

“Do not wait for a perfect beginning!”

Another answered from the stair:

“Nor call a beginning an ending!”

They climbed alongside the rushing souls. Dante did not try to outrun them. He moved with the purpose his own feet could carry, keeping the named task before him.

When the terrace opened at its upper boundary, an angel appeared with a light not borrowed from the sun.

Its blessing fell upon the travelers.

A fourth mark was lifted from Dante's brow.

Three remained.

Above them lay a quieter ledge.

The penitents here did not run. They lay face down upon the earth, their cheeks almost touching stone. Their hands were held close, as if relearning how to open them.

Dante stopped before one soul whose fingers trembled against the ground.

“What holds them here?” he asked.

Virgil answered, “The hunger to possess. Those who made the earth their treasure now learn how little can be carried upward in a closed hand.”

A distant voice spoke from the row of penitents.

“In life I counted what I owned. I did not count what owning cost.”

Dante looked down at his own hand.

Nothing rested in it.

Yet he felt how easily it might close.
