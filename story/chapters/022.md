## The Shared Weight

Dante did not remain only a watcher on the first terrace.

He stepped toward a soul bent beneath a stone so broad it seemed like a piece of the mountain itself laid upon a human back.

“May I help you bear it?” Dante asked.

The soul turned slightly. Even that motion seemed costly.

“You may walk beside me,” the penitent replied. “And if your shoulder can learn anything from nearness, let it learn.”

Dante moved beneath the edge of the stone.

He could not truly take the weight away. The burden belonged to the soul who carried it. Yet when he placed his shoulder under one corner, he felt how pride was answered here: not by speeches, but by pressure, slowness, and a changed posture.

The stone was not theatrical. It pressed down with patient certainty.

Dante took one step. Then another.

“In life,” the penitent said, breathing between the words, “I wanted height without measure. I wanted my own name to stand where no other name could stand beside it. Here the mountain teaches me the size I truly am.”

Dante looked toward the reliefs in the wall. Their images of humility did not mock the burdened souls. They accompanied them, like a lesson the body had to learn with the mind.

“You do not walk alone,” Dante said.

“No,” the penitent answered. “That is part of the cure.”

The words stayed with Dante.

So much in the Inferno had been fixed: storms repeating, gestures unable to change, defiance turned into its own prison. Here the burden had a direction. Each bent step belonged to the possibility of becoming different.

At length the stone bearer paused.

“That is enough,” the penitent said gently. “You cannot climb for me. But you have learned that a burden seen from outside is not the same as a burden felt from within.”

Dante stepped back. His shoulder still ached. The ache instructed him.

Virgil watched quietly.

“You have learned something here,” he said. “Not how to remove another's weight, but how to stand near it without pretending you know it fully.”

They continued along the terrace toward a stair cut into the mountain.

At its head an angel waited, radiant but gentle. Its wings stirred the air like a kindness too light to be called wind.

“Beati pauperes spiritu,” the angel sang.

As Dante passed, the angel touched his brow.

One of the seven marks vanished.

He did not see it disappear, but he felt an easing, as though a hidden knot had loosened.

They climbed.

The next terrace was narrower, a ledge against a cliff with little room for pride of stance. Voices moved through the air ahead of any visible speaker, naming generosity, blessing another's good, rejoicing in a light not one's own.

Then Dante saw the penitents.

They wore rough cloaks. Their eyelids were sewn shut with iron thread. Some leaned against others as they made their way along the cliff.

Here envy did not learn first through sight, but through hearing.

Virgil stopped beside him.

“The second terrace,” he said. “Where the eyes learn what they once refused to celebrate.”

Dante heard the voices continue along the cliff.

He did not yet know whose words they were, but he knew he would need to listen differently here.
