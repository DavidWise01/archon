## The Voice That Did Not Strike

The smoke of the third terrace closed around Dante until even the sleeve of Virgil's robe vanished.

He heard footsteps behind the shifting dark, then a voice, hoarse with exhaustion.

“Who walks here?”

The question landed sharply. Dante felt an answer rising in him—an accusation, a demand that the unseen speaker show himself.

Instead, he spoke softly.

“A traveler. I cannot see you. I am here with my guide.”

The voice did not reply at once.

Then came a quiet sound that might have been a breath of relief.

“I cannot see you either,” it said. “There is no advantage here in speaking as though we do.”

Dante kept one hand against the rock wall and waited. Smoke moved over his face like wool. He could not tell whether the other traveler stood close or far away.

“What do you seek?” Dante asked.

“To unlearn the moment when anger gives my tongue the shape of a weapon.”

They stood without seeing one another. The soul spoke of quarrels remembered long after their causes had faded; of words thrown at friends and strangers as though injury could create certainty. Dante listened, resisting the urge to settle a history he had not witnessed.

“The smoke does not make us harmless,” the penitent said. “It only makes us unable to aim. The work is to choose not to strike, even when our eyes return.”

Dante thought of his own first impulse.

“I felt anger before I knew whom I was angry with.”

“Then you have already seen something,” the soul replied.

A chant rose through the darkness, its voices joined without anyone needing to stand above the others. Virgil called Dante onward.

“Does gentleness make the path clear?” Dante asked.

“Not at once,” Virgil answered. “But it keeps us from destroying the path while we search.”

They climbed by the touch of the wall and the cadence of the singing. At length the smoke thinned. First a gray edge appeared; then a band of orange sky; then the stone steps of a higher terrace.

An angel stood where the darkness gave way.

Its wings moved the last smoke aside. With a touch upon Dante's brow, a third mark disappeared.

Four remained.

At the next turn, feet drummed against stone.

Souls ran along the terrace in urgent groups, calling examples of willing love to one another. None raced for a prize. No one stopped to be admired.

Dante stared after them.

“They run as though something precious will be lost,” he said.

“It will,” Virgil answered. “The hour in which good could have been done.”

The runners passed again, their words carrying through the evening air.

The fourth terrace lay open before them: a place where love delayed learned to move.
