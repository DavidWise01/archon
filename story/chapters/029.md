## The Gaze That Would Not Turn Away

The garden made no sound when Beatrice spoke.

Dante had crossed the fire. He had lost the seven marks. He had climbed farther than he once believed possible. Yet at the edge of the clear stream he understood that a journey can strip away punishment without finishing the work of truth.

“Look at me,” Beatrice said.

He raised his eyes.

He had remembered her as a light at a distance, a name to carry when the way was dark. Before him stood someone neither memory nor prize could contain. Her gaze was steady. She did not ask to be adored. She asked to be answered.

“Did you believe that reaching this garden would excuse the road you took?” she asked.

Dante wanted to speak of the forest, the beasts, the river, the mountain, and the flame. Each had changed him. But none could take his answer for him.

“No,” he said. “I wanted the height to prove I had become worthy of it.”

“And what do you see now?”

“That I can stand here and still be mistaken.”

For the first time he noticed the absence of Virgil.

He turned toward the place where his guide had stood. No familiar silhouette waited beneath the branches. Reason had led him to the threshold; it could not stand in his place before the truth.

Dante felt grief, sharp and clean.

Beatrice let him feel it without hurrying to quiet it.

“Do not make an idol of the one who guided you,” she said. “What he taught you must live in what you do.”

A white breeze moved over the stream. Its surface carried the reflections of trees, sky, and a face Dante could now bear to recognize as his own.

“I do not know how to undo my wandering,” he admitted.

“You cannot make it un-happen,” Beatrice answered. “But you can cease defending it. And then you can learn how to walk without repeating it.”

Dante met her gaze once more. This time he did not search for pardon in her eyes. He attended to what she had said.

The river before them was Lethe. Its waters promised release from the imprisoning hold of remembered sin—not the erasure of responsibility, not the destruction of the self that had learned.

At the water's edge, Beatrice waited.

The next crossing would ask what memory ought to release, and what wisdom must remain.
