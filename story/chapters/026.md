## The Open Hand

On the fifth terrace, the penitents lay face down against the stone.

Their bodies pressed toward the earth they had once clung to. Their hands seemed to relearn an older lesson one finger at a time: what is held too tightly can also hold the one who grasps it.

Dante stood still, looking at his own empty hand.

Virgil watched him. “You have named worthy tasks. Now ask what remains when giving is no longer easy.”

Dante closed his hand. Then opened it again.

“If I had only one coin left,” he said, “and another needed it more than I did, what would I be giving?”

“Not metal first,” Virgil answered. “Trust.”

A penitent nearby lifted his voice from the ground.

“In life I called my fear prudence. I told myself I was preserving what mattered. But the hand that never opened made a prison of caution.”

Dante knelt.

He imagined the last coin: not excess but the final thing one might use to secure comfort, pride or control.

“To offer the last coin,” Dante said, “would be to accept that worth is not measured by what remains mine.”

The penitent gave a rough laugh.

“Now say it again when there is truly something in your hand.”

Dante did not answer quickly. The mountain had taught him to distrust easy declarations.

He placed his open palm upon the stone.

“I would ask what is needed. And if giving would truly help, I would not call fear wisdom merely because it wears the same face.”

Virgil nodded. “That is nearer the truth.”

They moved among the prone penitents. Voices rose from the ground, recalling generosity and the freedom of relinquishment. Dante listened without romanticizing lack. Need was real. Hunger was real. Possession was not a perfect guard against fear.

At the stair, an angel greeted them with brightness that seemed to come through rather than merely from the air.

A fifth mark vanished from Dante's brow.

Two remained.

They climbed.

The next terrace announced itself first by fragrance.

Sweetness moved through the mountain air—fruit, water, green life in abundance. Dante heard running water before he saw it. Then a tree appeared, bending above a clear stream just beyond easy reach.

Souls moved beneath it with longing. They were gaunt, but their desire was not denied; it was being disciplined. Fruit hung above their reach.

Dante stopped.

“Here appetite is still alive,” he said.

“Yes,” Virgil answered. “But wanting something is not the same as needing to possess it.”

A breeze moved through the leaves. The scent deepened.

Dante felt thirst answer it.
