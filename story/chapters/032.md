## The Weight of an Invisible Crown

Mercury shone like a living sea of gold. Dante walked beside Beatrice while countless lights circled them, each carrying the memory of deeds accomplished during earthly life.

One light approached. Within it, Dante perceived a figure who had once held great power. The spirit spoke of laws, victories, rebuilding, and the difficult labor of governing.

“You accomplished much,” Dante said. “But tell me something: how can an action be good if the person performing it also desires glory?”

The light trembled.

“Does the farmer cease feeding the hungry because he enjoys seeing his name above the granary?”

“No,” Dante replied.

“Then you must learn to distinguish the deed from the hunger that accompanies it.”

Dante considered this.

“What if the hunger grows stronger than the deed?”

“Then the servant begins to serve his own reflection.”

Beatrice turned toward him.

“An intention is not simply what someone declares. It is also revealed by what they continue to do when the reward disappears.”

Dante remembered Piccarda. Her faithfulness had not depended upon the public recognition of her fidelity.

He understood something new.

A good deed could have mixed motives. Recognition did not automatically erase its benefit, but the desire for praise could corrupt future choices.

The spirit's light grew softer.

“Then I must ask not only whether I acted,” Dante said, “but what I would have done had no one been watching.”

Beatrice smiled.

Above them, Mercury's golden radiance began to give way to the warmer music of Venus.

The next heaven would ask what happens when love itself becomes the motive.
