## The Words Above the Gate

The gate stood open, taller than any tower Dante had known.

Its stones were black, but the words carved across the arch seemed to hold a pale fire. He could have crossed without reading them. Instead he stopped and read them all.

They spoke of the sorrowful city, of grief without rest, of a road made for justice and not for the comfort of travelers. At the bottom stood the terrible instruction: abandon every hope, you who enter.

Dante looked at Virgil.

“Does that mean there is no hope for us?”

“No,” Virgil answered. “It names the condition of those who remain here. It is not a command for the living to become what they witness.”

The difference mattered.

Dante understood that a warning could be true without swallowing the one who read it. He repeated its meaning slowly, not to obey its despair, but to remember where he had entered and why he must not remain.

Then the two poets crossed the threshold.

Behind them the gate did not close. Ahead lay a plain of distant cries, a wind carrying fragments of unfinished speech, and finally a broad black river. Lanterns moved along the far shore like embers caught in fog.

A narrow boat drifted near the bank.

Within it stood an old ferryman whose eyes burned with hard red light.

Virgil lowered his voice. “That is Charon. The river is Acheron.”

Dante looked at the water. It reflected no stars.

“Then what comes next?”

“A crossing,” said Virgil. “And the courage to hear what the river carries.”
