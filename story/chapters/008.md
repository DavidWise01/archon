## The Name Beneath the Rain

Rain fell without beginning or end.

It struck Dante's hood, drowned Virgil's footsteps, and filled every hollow of the third circle with black water. Cerberus moved beyond the haze, three mouths snarling at a hunger no meal could answer.

A shape rose from the mire.

“You from Florence,” said the muddy figure. “Do you remember your city?”

Dante knelt at the edge of the water. He could have called the shade glutton, sinner, or warning. Instead he offered the one thing the rain had not washed from him.

“My name is Dante,” he said. “What shall I call you?”

The man blinked, surprised that anyone had asked.

“Ciacco,” he answered. “It is what they called me when the streets still knew my feet.”

Dante repeated it carefully.

“Ciacco.”

For a moment, the rain seemed less loud—not because it had stopped, but because one voice had become distinct from the rest.

Ciacco spoke of Florence: of hungry factions, of friends who would come to despise their neighbors, and of a city capable of dividing even its memories. His words were not a complete account, and Dante did not pretend they were.

“Will it grow better?” Dante asked.

Ciacco turned his face toward the muddy sky. “That is a question for the living. I have given you only what I can see from here.”

The shade sank slowly beneath the rain.

Dante waited until the last ripple disappeared.

“You gave him your name,” Virgil observed.

“He had one already,” Dante said. “I wanted to hear it.”

They followed a descending path cut into the stone. The rain diminished to dripping water. Beneath their feet, a new sound began—a hard, regular grinding, followed by the crash of weight against weight.

A vast chamber opened ahead.

Two processions drove enormous stones in opposite directions. When the stones met, the people shouted questions that neither side stopped long enough to hear.

Far below, the burdens rolled again.

“Circle Four,” Virgil said. “Here the weight is not merely what they carried. It is what they could never set down.”
