## The Seven Signs

Dante stood before the gate and did not hurry.

The white step reflected him too clearly to be ignored. The dark step looked like a wound that had already begun to speak. The red step held a warmth that did not burn.

He looked at the angel's sword, then at the threshold beneath it.

“I do not want to pass through this as though it were only a door,” Dante said.

Virgil nodded. “Then remember what is happening.”

On the first step Dante saw himself. On the second he saw what in himself had to break. On the third he saw that brokenness was not the end of the ascent.

The angel bade him come forward.

Dante knelt.

With the point of the sword the angel traced seven marks upon his brow: **P P P P P P P**. Each stroke was light, but Dante felt every one.

“What are they?” he asked.

“The burdens that will be loosened as you climb,” Virgil answered. “Not erased by pretending they were never there. Lifted by passing through them truthfully.”

Dante touched his forehead but could not fully feel the shapes with his own hand.

He had to receive the knowledge before he could understand it.

Then the angel took two keys, one bright and one dark, and worked the lock. The hinges answered with a sound not violent but grave, as if the mountain itself recognized a lawful entry.

“Enter,” said the angel. “But remember: what opens may also be neglected. Do not look back in the old way.”

Virgil went first. Dante followed.

Inside, the air changed.

Morning light gathered into stone, path, and purpose. A hymn moved through the passage, not as a command but as atmosphere.

The way opened onto the first terrace.

Along the wall were carvings so vivid they seemed almost alive: a messenger bowing, a king dancing humbly before the ark, an emperor giving honor where honor was due.

Along the outer edge moved souls bent beneath enormous stones. Their bodies leaned forward under the weight, but their eyes turned toward the carvings, as though the burden forced them into an education they had once refused.

Dante stopped.

The seven marks on his brow had not yet lightened. He now understood they were not merely punishments waiting to happen. They were instructions for the climb.

Virgil stood beside him.

“This is the first terrace,” he said. “Here pride learns to look up by first being bent down.”

Dante watched one burdened soul take another step.

The stone did not vanish.

But the step was still made.
